## R2 - Three engines and my choice
Godot (GDScript 2D/3D) Godot is a free, lightweight, open-source engine built around reusable scenes and nodes, with easy-to-learn GDScript, strong 2D tools, and one-click export to many platforms. It has gained a lot of momentum. One 2026 roundup says it passed 110,000 GitHub stars in May 2026 and shipped Godot 4.6.2 in March. Comparisons generally agree that it excels at 2D and smaller 3D projects, while Unity leads in high-end 3D and VR/AR. Its scene and node system is simple, the editor is small, and there are no fees or royalties. Console export is the main gap, because it requires third-party porting services due to platform NDAs. https://godotengine.org

Unity (C# 2D/3D) is still the safest choice for a commercial team. It has the biggest asset store, the most tutorials and the most hiring availability, and it is strong on mobile and cross-platform builds. Unity’s Asset Store gives you a huge marketplace of ready-made tools, models, and plugins, so you can add features to your game without building everything from scratch. The downsides are that there are often several overlapping ways to do the same thing (input systems, render pipelines, MonoBehaviour vs. DOTS), not all fully supported, and that trust took a hit after the 2023 runtime-fee announcement. Pricing and terms change, so check Unity’s current pricing page before committing a team. https://unity.com

Stride (C# 3D) (formerly Xenko) is often described as the closest open-source analog to Unity, with Vulkan and DirectX 12 support and many modern features. Stride’s Game Studio editor lets you build scenes visually while writing all your game logic in standard C# with full access to .NET and NuGet packages. Its history is bumpy: it went through several rounds of funding, licensing and trademark issues that cost it momentum. It’s a good fit if you love C# and want a code-first, .NET-native engine. The tradeoff is that you’ll be on your own for tutorials, plugins and answers to problems. https://www.stride3d.net
